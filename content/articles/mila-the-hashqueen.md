**Mila Jansen**, widely known as **the Hash Queen**, is a Dutch-British entrepreneur, inventor, and author, born in 1944, whose work in the 1990s changed how hashish is made almost everywhere in the world. She invented the **Pollinator**, a mechanical drum that separates trichomes from dried cannabis through tumbling rather than hand-rubbing, and the **Ice-O-Lator**, a system of graduated filter bags that uses ice water to wash trichomes from plant material into what is now generically known as "bubble hash" or "ice water hash." Both inventions, along with her later motorized **Bubbleator**, became standard equipment across the legal and illegal cannabis industry alike, and *High Times* magazine recognized her with a Lifetime Achievement Award and a place on its inaugural list of the 100 Most Influential People in Cannabis.[^1][^2]

## Early life

Mila was born on December 5, 1944, in Liverpool, England, the daughter of an engineer for Shell Oil. Her family moved often in her childhood, living for periods in Indonesia, England, and the Netherlands before settling permanently in Holland when she was eleven.[^2] By her own account she had a happy early childhood that was upended when her mother suffered a stroke and was confined to a wheelchair.[^2]

Mila became pregnant as a young woman and was sent to a home for single mothers, an experience she later described in stark terms, recalling young women pressured to give up their children for adoption. She left the home with her daughter, Miloes, rather than surrender her.[^2]

Settling in central Amsterdam, Mila worked as a seamstress for a fashion studio, where she met designer Henk Koster. Together they opened a boutique called *Kink 22* in 1966, which quickly became a hub for the city's fashion avant garde, drawing figures such as Tina Turner and helping popularize bold looks like the miniskirt.[^1][^2] When Koster left to pursue film work in Italy, the space evolved into *Cleo de Merode*, a teahouse widely regarded as one of the first real precursors to Amsterdam's modern cannabis coffeeshops, although Mila has been careful to note that drugs there were shared among patrons rather than formally sold.[^1][^2]

*Cleo de Merode* became a gathering point for Amsterdam's expanding music, fashion, and art scene, but also drew complaints from neighbors and scrutiny from police and social services. Facing the threat of having her daughter taken from her, Mila closed the chapter quickly: with Miloes at her side and about six hundred U.S. dollars in her pocket, she set out overland for India.[^2]

## Nearly twenty years in India and Nepal

Mila and Miloes reached Goa on December 5, 1968, Mila's twenty-fourth birthday, after 102 days of overland travel by camper van, boat, bus, train, horseback, and raft.[^2] What followed was close to two decades living across the Indian subcontinent and Nepal, where Mila had three more children, Merl, Lali, and Chimed, largely raising them without support from the children's fathers.[^2]

She supported the family through a string of entrepreneurial hustles: shipping hashish to Europe hidden inside hollowed-out books, buying beaded and sequined blouses in Delhi to resell at a markup in Los Angeles and New York, and, in a venture that anticipated fair-trade fashion branding by decades, organizing a cooperative of roughly sixty seamstresses in the Himalayas to knit the chunky wool sweaters that became a must-have Western fashion item.[^2] Not every venture succeeded quietly; Mila has written about being caught in Frankfurt with a suitcase full of hashish and narrowly avoiding a serious sentence.[^2]

## Back to Amsterdam and the birth of the Pollinator

After nearly twenty years abroad, Mila returned to Europe with her youngest son, Chimed, settling back in Amsterdam, by then transformed into a global capital of cannabis culture. *High Times* had launched its first Cannabis Cup in 1988, and Amsterdam's coffeeshops were creating strong demand for higher-quality hashish and cannabis flower.[^2] Mila reconnected with old acquaintances from her years in Goa and moved into the growers' scene, a male-dominated world in which she found her position as a woman gave her, in her own telling, an unusual advantage in staying under the radar of the authorities.[^2]

The breakthrough came in 1994. Watching her children's laundry tumble in a dryer after a long day of manually shaking cannabis leaves over a sieve, Mila realized the two motions were essentially the same problem, and that the process of separating trichomes, the resin glands that contain most of a cannabis plant's cannabinoids and terpenes, could be mechanized.[^1] The result was the **Pollinator**, a rotating drum that uses controlled tumbling and screening to separate trichomes from dried plant material into dry-sift hashish, marketed under the tagline "hash from trash."[^1] Early customers were initially skeptical of hash made by machine, in part because the potency of mechanically sifted hashish outpaced what most smokers were used to at the time, well before the later boom in high-THC concentrates.[^1]

In 1997, Mila opened a dedicated Pollinator shop at Nieuwe Herengracht 25, near Amsterdam's Waterlooplein market, which became both her livelihood and a gathering place for cannabis travelers passing through the city.[^1] In 1998 she followed the Pollinator with the **Ice-O-Lator**, a set of graduated mesh filter bags that uses ice and water rather than dry tumbling to wash trichomes from cannabis material, producing what is now universally known as bubble hash or ice water hash.[^1] A later, motorized version of the same process, the **Bubbleator**, extended the same principle into a self-contained washing machine for hashish production.[^1]

## Later life, writing, and recognition

The success of the Pollinator, Ice-O-Lator, and Bubbleator made Mila financially secure, but she did not slow down. She opened a hotel themed entirely around cannabis, returned to India to find that towns like Manali had changed dramatically since her years there, and worked with her sister to make a documentary, *Mila's Journey*, exploring the cultural changes she found across the Himalayan region stretching from Dharamshala to Srinagar.[^2]

In 2018, Mila published an autobiography, *How I Became the Hash Queen*, covering her life from Liverpool and 1960s Amsterdam through her decades in India and Nepal to her career as an inventor. Reviewers have described the book as a fast-paced, colorful account that functions as much as a meditation on freedom and motherhood as a cannabis-industry memoir.[^2] *High Times* magazine has honored her with a Lifetime Achievement Award and included her among the inaugural class of its 100 Most Influential People in Cannabis, and she remains a fixture as a judge, speaker, and guest of honor at cannabis competitions and events.[^1][^2]

As of the mid-2020s, Mila remains active and publicly engaged with the cannabis community, including through her @milahashqueen social media presence, and is widely regarded as one of the few prominent women credited with a foundational technical invention in an industry that has historically been dominated by men.[^2]
